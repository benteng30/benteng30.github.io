Case study · Fall 2026 · UMass Amherst

Finding the bottleneck in a medical-device supply chain

Our student team worked with Beeken Biomedical, the company behind NuStat hemostatic gauze, on one question: can the current supply chain keep up as demand grows, and if not, what should the company do first? I owned the team's quantitative analysis.

Data analyst / Research lead

Recommendation deck and 90-day plan for Beeken, November 2026

My part

  • Analyzed demand, sales, inventory, and production data to pinpoint bottlenecks and capacity constraints.
  • Modeled 2x–3x demand-growth scenarios to size the production and inventory needs behind the team's recommendations.
  • Led the initial research, then took ownership of the team's quantitative analysis.

Skills used Capacity analysis · scenario modeling · unit conversion · inventory projection · data visualization

The question

Demand for NuStat was set to grow fast: the core product was expected to roughly double, and several new products were planned to launch on top of it. The company needed to know whether its suppliers could keep up, and where the first problem would show up.

The easy assumption is that the answer depends on how much demand grows. What we found is that it depends on one step in the chain.

Step 1

Map the whole chain

Every pad passes through the same sequence of suppliers, from yarn to the warehouse. Using Beeken's production data and the team's meetings with manufacturing partners, we listed every stage and what each one could handle per week.

Step 2

Put every stage in the same unit

The stages didn't measure capacity the same way. One step was measured in pounds of fabric per week, while demand was measured in pads. You can't compare them until they share a unit.

So we built a conversion table: for each product, how much fabric one pad uses, multiplied by its expected peak demand. That turned "pads needed" into "pounds needed," product by product.

What the conversion showed

Product mix matters as much as total volume. One larger product made up a small share of the pads but a much bigger share of the fabric, so a plan that only counts pads would miss where the pressure is.

Step 3

Find the stage that sets the pace

With everything in pads per week, we compared each stage's capacity to peak demand. Most stages had room to spare. One step, fabric processing at a single supplier, could handle only a fraction of what peak demand would need.

That reframed the problem. Adding capacity anywhere else would not help, because every pad still has to pass through that one critical step: fabric processing, handled by a single supplier between knitting and quality testing, with no quick backup, since qualifying a second supplier takes months.

Step 4

Project inventory under three scenarios

Next, we projected stock month by month at current capacity, under three demand scenarios:

  • Low: no new products launch.
  • Base: Beeken's own forecast.
  • High: demand comes in above forecast.

The useful result wasn't one prediction. It was that the conclusion held in every scenario: without more capacity at the slow step, stock eventually runs short. Higher demand only changes when. A fourth line shows the same model with the extra capacity the plan calls for.

Step 5

Check how long the obvious fix takes

The obvious fix is a second supplier for the slow step. But NuStat is an FDA-cleared device, and adding a new supplier means a risk assessment, process validation, and possibly a new 510(k) submission. That takes many months.

Putting that timeline next to the inventory projection showed a new supplier couldn't arrive in time to cover the near-term gap. The short-term answer had to come from the suppliers Beeken already had, with a second source started right away for the longer term.

What we recommended

A 90-day plan, each action with an owner at Beeken and a date:

  1. Negotiate more capacity at the slow step with the current supplier. This is the action that closes the gap.
  2. Send suppliers rolling forecasts so orders are steady instead of arriving in spikes.
  3. Set a monthly product allocation plan so limited fabric goes to the products that need it first.
  4. Build a safety buffer of the core product before demand peaks.
  5. Start qualifying a second source now, since it takes too long to help in the short term.

How to tell if it's working

We also gave Beeken a simple test. If the plan is working, the projected run-short date keeps moving later each month. If capacity at the slow step doesn't rise, or orders fall well below forecast, the plan needs to change.
